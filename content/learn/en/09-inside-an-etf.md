# You Bought an ETF. What Is Inside It?

Ten Lessons in Understanding Money · Lesson 9 · About 6–8 minutes · [한국어](../ko/09-inside-an-etf.md)

After reading a few company reports, a different question appears. If understanding one business takes this much work, how are you supposed to choose many of them?

Keeping up with dozens of companies while working and living your life is demanding. This is often where beginners encounter exchange-traded funds, or ETFs. On a trading screen, they look simple: enter a quantity and buy or sell.

Their contents may be much less simple. The same three-letter label can cover very different assets and strategies.

## The label describes a structure, not a promise

An ETF is a fund whose shares trade on an exchange. It pools money and invests according to a stated objective. Depending on the product, it may hold equities, bonds, or other exposures, including derivatives.

The label does not guarantee broad diversification. Some products concentrate on an industry or even target the performance of a single stock. The holdings, objective, and risks must be read separately. [Investor.gov ETF guide](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/investment-products/mutual-funds-and-exchange-traded-2)

An **index** is a measurement built using defined rules. An index fund seeks to track an index. Some ETFs do this; others use active management. Not every index fund is an ETF. The index is the reference measure, while the fund is the investment vehicle. [Investor.gov index fund guide](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/investment-products/mutual-funds-and-exchange-traded-4)

~~~text
INDEX
Rules for measuring a chosen market
              ↓ may be tracked by
ETF
A fund traded on an exchange
              ↓
Underlying assets and strategy
~~~

*Not every ETF tracks an index, and not every index fund is an ETF.*

## A hundred holdings can still be concentrated

Suppose a fictional ETF owns 100 companies. That sounds widely spread. But if its five largest holdings account for half the fund, those five can dominate the result.

| Fictional ETF X | Weight |
|---|---:|
| Five largest companies | 50% |
| Remaining 95 companies | 50% |
| Total | 100% |

*Invented weights to illustrate concentration, not an actual fund.*

Equal share quantities, equal money amounts, and market-capitalization weights produce different portfolios. Reading an index methodology helps explain why some businesses or sectors occupy much more space than others.

Owning several ETFs may also create overlap. Imagine half your portfolio is in ETF X and half in ETF Y. A particular company represents 10% of X and 6% of Y. Your overall exposure to that company is 8%.

> 50% × 10% + 50% × 6% = 8%

The account contains two products, but part of the underlying exposure is repeated. Looking through the labels reveals more than counting the number of funds.

## What risk are you spreading?

Owning several companies reduces dependence on the fate of a single business. But companies in the same industry may react to the same economic forces. Broad stock-market declines remain possible too.

Diversification has several dimensions: companies, industries, countries, and asset classes. The point is to understand concentration, rather than collect every possible category.

Your work can be part of the picture. If much of your investment wealth is in your employer’s shares, both your salary and portfolio may depend on the same company. That connection may matter more than the number of positions listed in the account.

An ETF also does not choose your personal asset allocation for you. A broad equity ETF is still exposed to equities. Whether it fits money needed next month is a separate question from whether its holdings are diversified.

## Small costs continue year after year

Funds incur management and operating costs. Trading commissions, bid–ask spreads, currency conversion, and taxes may add to the investor’s total cost. A low stated management fee does not settle the entire calculation.

Costs also reduce the amount left to compound. [SEC explanation of investment fees and expenses](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/general-resources/news-alerts/alerts-bulletins/investor-bulletins/updated)

For illustration, suppose 1 million won grows at 5% annually before costs. Simplify one investment’s net return to 4.8% and another’s to 4.0%. After twenty years, the amounts are about 2.55 million and 2.19 million won.

This subtracts a cost allowance directly from the annual return. It is not a reproduction of daily fund accounting or a forecast for any product. It simply makes a recurring difference visible.

Before comparing costs, make sure the products offer comparable exposure and risk. A cheaper fund owning entirely different assets is not necessarily a substitute. Match the investment purpose first, then examine costs and how effectively the fund delivers its objective.

## The traded price matters as well

A fund’s net asset value, or NAV, reflects assets less liabilities. Dividing by the relevant share count gives NAV per share. The price at which ETF shares trade can differ from that value.

There is also a gap between prices buyers offer and prices sellers request: the bid–ask spread. Trading conditions can depend on the liquidity of the underlying assets, activity in the ETF, and whether relevant overseas markets are open.

A market order does not specify the execution price. A limit order sets a price boundary, but it may not execute if no matching trade is available. Neither order type guarantees every desired outcome.

Reading the default settings on a trading screen is therefore part of understanding the investment. A familiar-looking interface can make a consequential choice feel routine.

The fund’s tracking result also deserves attention. For a comparable index fund, examine how its return differs from the stated benchmark, using the same period and the same treatment of distributions. Fees explain some differences, but implementation, taxes, and cash holdings can also matter.

## Read the small words after the name

Leveraged and inverse ETFs often target a multiple of, or the opposite of, a **daily** return. Their return over several days need not equal that multiple of the benchmark’s cumulative return. [SEC guide to leveraged and inverse ETFs](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/general-resources/news-alerts/alerts-bulletins/investor-alerts/sec)

Imagine an index rising 10% on day one and falling approximately 9.09% on day two. It moves from 100 to 110 and back to 100.

An idealized fund delivering exactly twice each daily return rises from 100 to 120, then falls approximately 18.18% to 98.18. The difference appears even before fees. Each day applies its percentage to a changed balance.

This makes a daily leveraged fund more than a faster version of an ordinary long-term holding. Its daily objective, derivatives exposure, and potential for amplified losses require separate understanding.

For practice, open the official documentation for one ETF. Write down its objective, largest holdings and weights, total costs, and currency exposure. Then compare its contents with anything you already own.

You do not need a long list of fund names to make progress. Start by explaining where the money inside one product is actually invested. Once that is clear, the reason for adding another product becomes easier to examine.
