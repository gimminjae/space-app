# What Do You Own When You Buy a Share?

Ten Lessons in Understanding Money · Lesson 6 · About 6–8 minutes · [한국어](../ko/06-what-a-share-means.md)

The Corner Bakery often sells out before lunch. Its owner wants a larger oven and more space, but the equipment and deposit require money she does not have.

She could borrow. She could also sell part of the business to someone willing to share its future results.

The Corner Bakery is fictional. Real listed companies are much more complicated, but a small business helps reveal what a stock-price screen can hide. A **share** is a unit of ownership in a company. Buying one changes your relationship to the business: you participate in its economic outcome rather than merely purchasing its products. [Investor.gov stock guide](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/investment-products/stocks)

## Ownership is not a daily slice of sales

Imagine the bakery has 10,000 shares, all with identical rights, and you own 100. Your ownership stake is 1%.

This does not entitle you to 1% of the cash register every evening. Revenue must pay for ingredients, wages, rent, and other costs. Even profit need not be distributed in full. The company may retain money for equipment, expansion, or resilience.

If a dividend is declared, payment follows the relevant decisions and share rights. Many ordinary shares carry voting rights, but share classes differ. Preferred shares and structures with unequal voting rights are examples of why the precise terms matter.

Ownership also has a position in the queue when things go badly. Creditors have contractual claims. Shareholders have a residual claim on what remains after obligations are met. If nothing remains in liquidation, shareholders can lose their entire investment. Borrowing to invest or using derivatives introduces additional risks beyond an ordinary fully paid share purchase.

## Does your purchase send money to the company?

When a company issues new shares to investors, it receives proceeds after issuance costs. It can use the money for expansion, debt repayment, or other stated purposes.

An ordinary purchase of existing shares through a brokerage app usually works differently. You buy from another holder. Your money goes to the seller, not into the company’s bank account.

~~~text
NEW SHARE ISSUE
Investor ── capital ──▶ Company

TRADING EXISTING SHARES
Buyer ── payment ──▶ Existing holder
Buyer ◀── shares ── Existing holder
~~~

*A simplified distinction between issuance and secondary trading; intermediaries and settlement are omitted.*

The secondary market still matters to companies. Investors may be more willing to provide capital when they expect to be able to sell their holdings later. Market valuations can also influence subsequent fundraising. But pressing “buy” is not usually a direct payment to the business.

## A low share price does not establish cheapness

Suppose the bakery has 10,000 shares trading at 10,000 won each. Its market capitalization is 100 million won.

> Market capitalization = share price × relevant outstanding share count

We use a simple share structure here, without treasury-share complications.

Another bakery has shares priced at 5,000 won, but 100,000 shares outstanding. Its market capitalization is 500 million won.

| Fictional company | Share price | Share count | Market capitalization |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Corner Bakery | KRW 10,000 | 10,000 | KRW 100 million |
| Riverside Bakery | KRW 5,000 | 100,000 | KRW 500 million |

The second share has a lower sticker price, but the market assigns a larger value to the company’s total equity. You need to know what fraction the slice represents.

Market capitalization is itself only an equity value. Comparing entire businesses may require examining debt and cash as well. For now, the essential distinction is between the price of one share and the value assigned to all the equity.

A stock split illustrates this neatly. If each share becomes two and the theoretical price halves, the ownership percentage and total value are unchanged at that instant. Actual trading can introduce other price movements, but cutting a slice in two does not double the bakery.

## Better results can still disappoint

The Corner Bakery earns more this year than last year. Its share price can still fall.

Perhaps investors expected a much larger improvement and had already paid a price reflecting that expectation. The reported result is good in isolation but disappointing relative to what was priced in.

A market price reflects expectations about future cash generation, uncertainty around those expectations, available alternatives, and buyers’ and sellers’ willingness to trade. It is not a mechanical display of today’s sales.

Nor does every daily move need a satisfying explanation. In a thinly traded market, a small order can have an outsized effect. Money moving between funds can affect prices independently of a company’s latest news. Stories written after a move may be plausible interpretations rather than established causes.

It helps to keep two separate notes: what changed in the business, and what changed in its market price. Over time, you can investigate their relationship without assuming that every movement has an obvious business explanation.

## Count dividends and price changes together

Imagine buying a share for 10,000 won. A year later it is worth 10,500, and you have received a cash dividend of 300 won. Excluding taxes, costs, and dividend reinvestment, total return is 8%.

> (10,500 − 10,000 + 300) ÷ 10,000 = 8%

The price gain alone is 5%. The dividend contributes the remaining 3 percentage points.

If the share instead falls to 9,000 won, the same 300-won dividend leaves a total return of −7%. Receiving cash does not eliminate the possibility of a loss.

A dividend also moves cash out of the company. Other things equal, the value remaining inside the business falls accordingly. Actual trading on the ex-dividend date reflects other forces too, but the payment is not wealth created without an offset. You need to ask whether the business can sustainably earn the money it distributes.

Issuing new shares changes another part of ownership. If you own 100 of 10,000 shares and the company issues 2,000 more without your participation, your stake falls from 1% to about 0.83%.

Yet the company also receives new capital. A lower percentage does not automatically mean an equivalent economic loss. The issue price and what the company does with the proceeds matter. Financing that creates valuable capacity is different from repeated fundraising that merely covers persistent losses.

## Cover the company name for a moment

A familiar brand can give you a starting point. It cannot settle the investment case. A company can make excellent products while its shares trade at a price that leaves little room for disappointment.

Before deciding whether a stock is attractive, describe how the business earns money in one plain sentence. Then write down a cost that could increase and a condition under which the business might struggle.

“People will keep eating bread” is broad. “Can this shop cover rent and wages if nearby office traffic falls?” gives you something to investigate.

You do not need the answer immediately. The unanswered question tells you what to read next: revenue sources, cost structure, debt obligations, or the notes explaining management’s plans.

There is no Corner Bakery stock to buy and no correct purchase price hidden in this lesson. In the next chapter, we will open its accounts. More customers are encouraging, but we still need to see whether sales become profit and whether profit becomes cash.
