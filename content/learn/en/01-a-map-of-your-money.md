# The Paycheck Arrived. Where Did the Money Go?

Ten Lessons in Understanding Money · Lesson 1 · About 7–9 minutes · [한국어](../ko/01-a-map-of-your-money.md)

On Friday evening, a paycheck notification appeared on Jiyun’s phone. She opened a food delivery app. After a long week, a slightly expensive dinner seemed reasonable. Two days later, her credit card payment left the account. Rent and the phone bill followed. By Wednesday, the comfortable balance had begun to look rather lonely.

Jiyun is a fictional reader we will follow through this course. She takes home 3 million Korean won a month. She does not think of herself as extravagant, yet her savings never seem to grow. When she looks for investing advice, she finds plenty of people discussing stocks. Almost nobody starts with the question she needs answered: how much can she actually afford to invest?

To answer that, we need a wider view than an account balance. Money already in the account, money expected next month, and money already promised to someone else are different things.

## Some of the balance has already been spent

Suppose you have 1 million won in your account, with an 800,000-won card payment due next week. You do not have 1 million won available for new plans. Much of it already has a destination.

Finding these commitments is the first job of a budget. Recording what comes in and goes out reveals your **cash flow**. Timing matters as well as amounts. If rent falls due before payday, you can run short for several days even when your monthly income covers your monthly expenses.

Jiyun groups three months of transactions into a rough monthly picture. These figures are invented for the lesson; they are not suggested spending proportions.

| Monthly item | Amount, KRW | What it tells her |
|---|---:|---|
| Take-home pay | 3,000,000 | Money actually received |
| Housing, utilities, phone | 950,000 | Recurring commitments |
| Food, transport, essentials | 700,000 | Variable everyday costs |
| Eating out, hobbies, subscriptions | 350,000 | Spending she can reconsider |
| Loan principal and interest | 250,000 | Two different kinds of payment |
| Provision for annual expenses | 200,000 | Bills that do not arrive monthly |
| Money remaining | 550,000 | Available for reserves and goals |

The final line is not automatically an investing allowance. Jiyun still needs an emergency reserve and money for a future move. Choosing what the remainder should do comes after finding out how much remains.

There is also a small bookkeeping trap here. If you record a card purchase when you make it, do not count the later card payment as a second purchase. Pick one consistent method. A simple record you maintain will tell you more than an elaborate spreadsheet you abandon.

## An unusual month can be entirely predictable

In an ordinary month, Jiyun’s budget appears to work. Then an annual insurance premium arrives, or several family occasions fall close together. The budget slips into deficit. Each time, she tells herself that this month was exceptional.

Repeated exceptions belong in the plan. If predictable annual expenses total 2.4 million won, setting aside 200,000 won each month prepares for them. When the balance in that reserve falls after paying an annual bill, the plan is doing its job.

An unexpected loss of income is different. So is a repair whose timing and size could not reasonably have been anticipated. An emergency fund is a separate reserve for this kind of disruption. The US Consumer Financial Protection Bureau describes its size as dependent on individual circumstances, rather than one universal target. [CFPB emergency fund guide](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/an-essential-guide-to-building-an-emergency-fund/)

Several months of essential expenses can be a useful starting point for thinking. It is not a requirement that fits every household. Someone with irregular seasonal income faces different risks from someone with a dependable salary. Dependants, available support, and likely urgent expenses also change the calculation. A first milestone might simply be enough to absorb one plausible disruption.

Its purpose should guide where the reserve sits. Can you access it when needed? Could its value have fallen sharply on the day you need it? A higher quoted return may be a poor trade if withdrawal carries a large penalty or the balance depends on a volatile market price.

## A salary is a flow; wealth is a balance

Cash flow describes movement over a period. **Net worth** describes your position on a particular date.

> Net worth = assets you own − liabilities you owe

Imagine deposits of 5 million won, investments worth 3 million, and a refundable housing deposit of 20 million. Assets total 28 million won. With a loan balance of 10 million, net worth is 18 million. The example assumes the housing deposit is recoverable; its actual collectability and return date need separate attention.

This distinction explains why loan payments deserve two labels. Repaying 100,000 won of principal reduces cash by 100,000 and reduces debt by the same amount. Taken alone, that transaction leaves net worth unchanged. Paying 10,000 won of interest reduces cash without reducing principal, so net worth falls by 10,000.

Accessibility matters too. A large housing deposit cannot necessarily pay next week’s medical bill. A household can have positive net worth and very little usable cash.

~~~text
OVER A MONTH             ON ONE DATE
Income                   Assets
  ↓                        −
Spending                 Liabilities
  ↓                        =
Money for goals          Net worth
~~~

*Two views of the same finances: movement through time and a position at one moment.*

## Every goal comes with a date

Jiyun considers giving all 550,000 won of her monthly remainder one label: savings. Then she writes down what it is meant to cover. An emergency reserve. A move in two years. Living costs much further into the future.

Those dates change the choices available. Money needed soon cannot wait indefinitely for a market recovery. Money that can remain untouched for longer may have more options. We will return to this distinction when we discuss stocks and bonds.

Separate accounts may help, but the labels come first. You can track several purposes within one account if you know how much must remain for each. Opening extra accounts without a plan can add complexity without adding clarity.

Debt belongs on the same page. Write down its interest rate and repayment conditions. That prepares you to compare the interest saved by repayment with the uncertain return from investing. Also check how much cash must remain until the next payday. Using every available won to repay a loan can backfire if an ordinary bill then forces you to borrow again at a higher cost.

If income is irregular, an average month may hide a difficult one. A few unusually busy periods can pull the average upward. Check whether essential costs remain manageable during quieter months, and decide what to retain when income is stronger. Two households with the same annual income can need different cash buffers because their payment schedules differ.

## Three lines are enough to begin

A transaction history can feel like a list of mistakes. Every delivery meal or impulse purchase seems to demand an explanation. But a useful budget helps with the next decision; it does not need to put the previous month on trial.

Jiyun cancels a subscription she barely uses and keeps dinner with friends. She starts with spending that provides little satisfaction. If income cannot cover essentials, a few skipped coffees may not solve the problem. Housing costs, debt terms, or opportunities to improve income deserve attention at a different scale.

For today, write three lines: money actually coming in, money already committed, and money you want to keep for the future. Use recent transactions to make a rough estimate. Mark any large payments due within the next 30 days. Revise the page after the next payday.

Jiyun’s salary is still 3 million won. What has changed is that the balance now carries names: rent, insurance, an unexpected medical bill, a move two years away. With those names visible, she can begin to see what is truly available.
